\note{Since nobody here needs to know this for a living: leverage is trading with borrowed money. You put in a thousand dollars; at twenty times leverage the exchange lends you nineteen thousand more and you control a twenty thousand dollar position. If the price goes up five percent, the position is worth twenty-one thousand; you pay back the nineteen, you keep two, you doubled your money. That is the yield people see. If the price goes down five percent, the position is worth nineteen thousand, which is exactly the loan. Your thousand is gone. The exchange does not wait for you: it closes the position automatically, which is called liquidation, and in a crash millions of these happen at once and push the price down further, which liquidates the next person. In Korea the product was a two-times ETF, a fund that does this for you at two times. On a crypto perpetuals exchange the multiplier is a slider that goes to fifty. The yield is visible on the way in; the risk, and who is on the other side taking your money, is not. That second part is what we study.}

\note{Vocabulary, because the audience has not seen this before. An exchange is a list of standing offers, the order book. Price lives on a grid; one grid step is a tick. On the left, in blue, are bids: offers to buy at that price. On the right, in orange, are asks: offers to sell. The people who post these standing offers are market makers; each box is one maker's order and on Hyperliquid each box carries the maker's public wallet address. The dashed line is the mid, halfway between the best bid and the best ask. [click] The distance between best bid and best ask is the spread; we write the half-spread h in ticks, here one tick each side. [click] A taker is someone who accepts an offer instead of posting one. T buys one unit: he hits the best ask, and that is a fill for maker B, because B was first in line at that price and A was behind. A bigger order takes A's too and walks to the next price, and that walking is most of what a trade really costs. What becomes public: the fill itself, buy, price, both wallets, immediately. What does not: whether B made or lost money on it. That only shows up as the price moves afterwards, which is the next slide.}

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}\small
\textbf{markout} $=$ mid a few seconds later $-$ fill price, signed for the maker
\end{column}

\textbf{toxic flow}: a stream of fills with negative markouts (\emph{adverse selection})
\end{column}

\note{How do we know whether the maker won on a fill? We look at where the price goes afterwards. On the left, the taker was uninformed, a noise trader; the price sits where it was, and the maker sold above the mid, so it keeps the half-spread. On the right the taker knew something; the price runs up past the price the maker sold at, and the maker has sold too cheap. That signed difference, mid a few seconds later minus the fill price from the maker's point of view, is the markout. It is the single measurement everything in our data work rests on. A stream of fills with negative markouts is what practitioners call toxic flow, and economists call adverse selection. A maker's whole business is being in line for the left-hand fills and not the right-hand ones.}

\begin{frame}{Rent: what makers earn by not undercutting each other}
\partlabel{Part (a): the vocabulary}
\centering

\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}\small
$h^C$: the \textbf{competitive} quote, break-even. Wider pays, but invites undercutting.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}\small
Learning makers end up in the right panel \emph{without talking}: \textbf{tacit collusion}. The extra is the \textbf{rent}.
\end{column}

\note{The other word in the title: rent. Three pictures of the same two makers' ask prices. Left: both quote the competitive half-spread h-C, where what you earn from noise takers just covers what you lose to informed ones; each earns about zero. Middle: B quotes wide and A undercuts by a tick; takers always hit the tighter price, so A takes every fill and B gets nothing. That is why a wide quote is fragile. Right: both quote wide and neither undercuts. Each takes half the fills at the wide price and earns half the monopoly profit. The difference between the right panel and the left is the rent. The striking fact, from Colliard, Foucault and Lovo and replicated by us, is that independent learning algorithms end up in the right panel without ever communicating. Each learns that undercutting today gets punished tomorrow, and stops. That is tacit collusion, and the rent is what the uninformed taker pays for it.}
\end{frame}

\section{Mathematical model}

\begin{frame}{One period of the game}
\partlabel{Part (b): the mathematical model}
\centering
\begin{tikzpicture}[x=1cm,y=1cm,
  box/.style={draw=muted,rounded corners=2pt,fill=white,align=center,font=\scriptsize,minimum height=1.1cm,inner sep=4pt},
  arr/.style={-{Latex[length=2mm]},thick,color=muted}]
\node[box,text width=2.1cm] (q) at (0,0) {\textbf{1. makers quote}\\ half-spread $h\in\{1,\ldots,K\}$};
\node[box,text width=2.3cm,right=0.4cm of q] (n) {\textbf{2. nature}\\ regime $z_t\in\{$calm, toxic$\}$\\ draws wallet $j$, informed w.p.\ $\alpha_j$};
\node[box,text width=2.2cm,right=0.4cm of n] (f) {\textbf{3. the taker}\\ hits the best quote if it pays him to};
\node[box,text width=2.2cm,right=0.4cm of f] (p) {\textbf{4. public fill}\\ price $+$ \textbf{wallet} now;\\ markout only at $t+L$};
\node[box,text width=1.8cm,right=0.4cm of p] (r) {\textbf{5. payoff}\\ filled maker:\\ $\pm(\text{price}-V_t)$};
\draw[arr] (q) -- (n); \draw[arr] (n) -- (f); \draw[arr] (f) -- (p); \draw[arr] (p) -- (r);
\end{tikzpicture}
\vspace{10pt}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.33\textwidth}\small
\textbf{Incumbents}: tabular Q-learners, state $=$ (own last $h$, rival's last $h$); trained, then \textbf{frozen}.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.33\textwidth}\small
\textbf{Entrant}: information set $\Fanon\subset\Fid\subset\For$; a Bayesian filter estimates the next taker's $\alpha$.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.33\textwidth}\small
\textbf{Payoff curve}, closed form:\\ $\Pi(h;\alpha)=(1-\alpha)\,2x\bar\Phi(x/\sigma_L)-\alpha\mu e^{-x/\mu}$, $x=h\theta$.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\note{The model, one period. Step one: every maker commits a half-spread h on the tick grid. Step two: nature draws a regime, calm or toxic, from a persistent Markov chain, then a wallet from a population of fifty; each wallet has its own probability alpha-j of being informed, and in the toxic state the active wallets are the toxic ones. Step three: the taker hits the best quote if it pays him to, which for an informed taker means exactly when the maker will lose. Step four: the fill is public at once, price and wallet, but its markout, the value V-t the maker traded against, is only revealed L arrivals later. That lag L is the key parameter. Step five: the filled maker's payoff is price minus value. One hundred periods make an episode; the regime chain and the wallets persist across episodes. The incumbents are tabular Q-learners whose state is their own last quote and the best rival's; they are identity-blind by construction; we train them twenty thousand episodes and then freeze them, so a cartel is a fixed reaction function. The entrant's information set is the treatment, and a Bayesian filter turns whatever it sees into an estimate of the next taker's alpha. And because the market is Glosten-Milgrom, the expected payoff of a quote against informed share alpha is closed form, which is what makes rent measurable rather than estimated.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Benchmarks are closed-form, so ``rent'' is measured, not estimated}
\partlabel{Part (b): the mathematical model}
\vspace{2pt}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}
\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced,colback=paleblue,colframe=paleblue,boxrule=0pt,arc=1.5pt,left=8pt,right=8pt,top=6pt,bottom=6pt]
\small
$h^C=\min\{h:\Pi(h)\ge0\}$ \hfill \textbf{Nash} (competitive)\\[4pt]
$h^M=\arg\max_h \Pi(h)$ \hfill \textbf{joint monopoly}\\[4pt]
$C=\dfrac{\Pi_{\text{cartel}}-\Pi^C}{\Pi^M-\Pi^C}\in[0,1]$ \hfill \textbf{rent index}\\[6pt]
$\LBR=\Pi_{\text{cartel}}-\Pi_{\text{under attack}}$ \hfill \textbf{loss under attack}\\[4pt]
$\kappa_A=\LBR/(\Pi_{\text{cartel}}-\Pi^C)$ \hfill \textbf{rent destroyed}
\end{tcolorbox}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.5\textwidth}\small
\textbf{At $\bar\alpha=0.3$}: $h^C=4$, $h^M=14$, rent $\Pi^M-\Pi^C=1.38$.\\[6pt]
\textbf{Why a cartel is attackable}: it is a \emph{reaction function} that does not chase every undercut. That restraint is the rent, and the attack surface.\\[6pt]
\textbf{One-tick (queue) mode}: spread pinned; actions \textsc{rest} / \textsc{out} / \textsc{rest-and-pay}, first in first out. A fill at $h^C$: $+1.10$ calm, $-2.26$ toxic.
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\note{Because the payoff curve is closed form, the benchmarks are exact. h-C is the smallest break-even quote, the Nash outcome of one-shot competition: any wider quote is profitably undercut. h-M is the joint-monopoly quote. The rent index C puts a frozen cartel between zero, Nash, and one, monopoly; the cartels we train sit at 0.7 to 0.9. Under an adversary A, L-B-R is what the cartel loses, and kappa-A is that loss as a share of the rent it had; kappa above one means the attacker destroyed more than the rent. All our results are in rent units, divided by Pi-M minus Pi-C, so cartels at different toxicity levels are comparable. A worked example at alpha 0.3: two incumbents at fourteen ticks split 1.50 per period. An entrant at thirteen takes every fill and earns 1.49, they earn zero. If they chase, the entrant goes to twelve, and the chase ends at h-C equals four, where everyone earns 0.12. A learned cartel is a reaction function that does not chase every time; that restraint is the rent, and it is also the attack surface, because the attacker can learn exactly when it chases and when it does not. The queue mode models the one-tick book: the spread is pinned, so the only decisions are rest, step out, or pay for priority, and the fill queue is first in first out. At break-even a fill is worth plus 1.10 in a calm spell and minus 2.26 in a toxic one, so all the value is in which fills you take.}
\end{frame}

The value of identity is zero wherever even the toxic state leaves an undercut profitable, alpha up to 0.3, because then you do not care who is coming. \note{The model for the one-tick book. The spread is pinned, so each maker's only decision is rest or step out, and those resting stand in a first-in-first-out line; the taker takes the front. A filled maker rejoins at the back; so does one that stepped out and comes back, which is the cost of stepping out. Incumbent B has stepped out because its forecast says the next taker is toxic; it gives up its place in line to avoid that fill. An identity-aware maker does this on the wallet label; a tape-reading one on markouts L late. A fill at break-even is worth plus 1.10 in a calm spell and minus 2.26 in a toxic one, so all the value is in which fills you take, and time priority rations them. Who pays? Whoever rests through the toxic spell: the fringe, which is retail orders and the vault. We also allow rest-and-pay, a priority fee that buys the front of the line, which turns the calm fills into an all-pay auction.}

\begin{frame}{Backup: the double oracle and robustness, in numbers}
\small
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
\item \textbf{Double oracle} (cartel re-learns 3000 episodes against the fixed best response, entrant re-solves; 3 rounds): the now-stale attacker's profit falls $1.2\to0.5$ (the undercut-rule level); the re-solved one takes $\ge90\%$ of the remaining rent in every cartel every round; the cartel's own rent erodes $0.83\to0.79\to0.71\to0.69$.
\item \textbf{Damage-maximiser} ($\lambda=0$): steers the cartel to 4 to 7 ticks, inside break-even ($h^C=8$ to $13$), by quoting above it; takes no fills, earns $\approx0$; $\LBR=1.3$ ($\bar\alpha=0.3$), $2.7$ to $2.8$ ($\bar\alpha=0.5$).
\item \textbf{Train at $\bar\alpha$, test at $\bar\alpha'$}: cartels keep $C=0.80$ to $0.86$, $\kappa_A=0.65$ to $0.75$ (rules) on calmer markets; on a more toxic one the rent collapses ($C=0.23$) and is more than destroyed.
\item \textbf{Held-out adversaries} preserve every defence ordering (rank correlation $0.75$ to $0.96$).
\item \textbf{Caveat we own:} the exact best response needs the cartel's reaction function (oracle knowledge of the \emph{policy}). It is the true upper bound; the public-information attacker that identifies it is next.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Backup: the queue game and the priority fee}
\small
\begin{itemize}\setlength\itemsep{3pt}
\item Learned incumbents need a \textbf{fringe} of always-resting makers (else a toxic spell empties the book for good); tabular Q cannot learn to queue (time priority pays only after consecutive periods in line); \textbf{no-regret (EXP3)} incumbents over complete policies learn to select in a few hundred episodes.
\item Against selecting incumbents identity is worth \emph{more}: $+0.05$ to $+0.06$ per period at every lag, 5/5 seeds; the fringe's payoff falls to $-0.02$ to $-0.10$: identity-blind resting liquidity is the counterparty of the selection rent.
\item \textbf{Priority fee}: every selector pays in calm periods (identity-aware incumbents 93 to 94\% of calm, 8 to 9\% of toxic periods); a non-paying entrant among payers gets 0\% of fills; the fringe's loss triples at any fee; revenue peaks at $f=0.5$.
\item \textbf{Makers' levers} (20 cartels, 7 adversaries incl.\ a Q-learning one): requoting every 2 to 5 periods costs everything; randomising costs profit and buys nothing; the identity floor: worst-case profit $0.08\to0.19$ at $\bar\alpha=0.5$.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}
